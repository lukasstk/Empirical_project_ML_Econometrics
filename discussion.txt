================================================================================
DISCUSSION: SHORTCOMINGS AND THREATS TO IDENTIFICATION
================================================================================

1. SELECTION BIAS / FAILURE OF CONDITIONAL IGNORABILITY (main shortcoming)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The DML-PLR estimates rest on conditional ignorability: given the controls
W, policy status must be as good as randomly assigned. The placebo test
(announced-but-not-yet-active city-years) rejects this. Both placebo
coefficients are large, positive and significant (cp_pre = +0.33,
lez_pre = +0.39): before any charge is collected or any vehicle is banned,
future adopters already emit 33-39 log points more than W predicts.

Cities do not adopt congestion pricing or a low-emission zone at random --
they adopt them in response to the very outcome under study. High-emission,
congested cities face public pressure to act; low-emission cities have
nothing to regulate. This "selection on the outcome" is not captured by the
included covariates, so part of the pre-existing emission gap is loaded
onto the treatment coefficients.

Direction of the bias is identifiable from the placebo signs: UPWARD for
both policies. Consequences:
- The positive LEZ "effect" (+0.29) is approximately the size of the LEZ
  pre-gap (+0.39) and is therefore plausibly pure selection. A causal
  reading of "LEZs raise CO2 by a third" would be wrong.
- The CP estimate (-0.18) is an upper bound (in the algebraic sense); the
  true effect is likely more negative, roughly -0.5 log points if the
  pre-gap carries through unchanged.
- The strongly negative interaction and the joint effect are less clearly
  affected: for the bias to produce a spurious negative synergy, cities
  adopting BOTH policies would have to be negatively selected, which the
  placebo design cannot test separately.

2. WHY THE CONTROL SET CANNOT FIX THIS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The lasso (and any other nuisance learner) selects variables that PREDICT
well, not variables that close backdoor paths. If the confounder is a
time-invariant or slowly-trending city attribute (e.g., a persistent
car-dependence or congestion level that is only imperfectly proxied by
density, transit score, and the other observables), no amount of flexible
learning on the SAME covariates recovers it. The estimator checks in 04
(learners, folds, control sets) all vary the ESTIMATOR; none of them can
repair a violated identifying assumption -- and indeed they all reproduce
the same biased pattern.

The design also uses no within-city variation: the partially linear model
pools cross-sectional and temporal variation, with year and country dummies
but no city fixed effects. Any city-specific level difference correlated
with adoption goes straight into the treatment coefficients. Natural
remedies -- city fixed effects (within transformation or Mundlak device) or
conditioning on pre-treatment outcome levels/lags -- interact awkwardly with
lasso-based selection (many incidental parameters, mechanical correlation
between lagged outcomes and treatment timing) and were left outside the
scope of this project; they are the obvious next step.

3. ANTICIPATION VS. SELECTION CANNOT BE FULLY SEPARATED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The placebo period (announcement to implementation, ~1.5 years) could in
principle also contain anticipation behavior (e.g., replacing a diesel car
before the LEZ starts). Anticipation would push the placebo DOWNWARD, so
the observed POSITIVE coefficients cannot be explained by it; selection
dominates. But because every city that discussed a policy eventually
implemented one, the data contain no "announced but never implemented"
cities that would allow separating the two mechanisms cleanly.

4. TREATMENT-EFFECT HOMOGENEITY AND STAGGERED ADOPTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The PLR imposes a single constant coefficient per treatment (on the log
scale). Adoption is staggered over time, and the modern
difference-in-differences literature shows that pooled two-way comparisons
can weight cohort effects in unintended ways when effects vary over time
or across cohorts (e.g., effects that grow as enforcement tightens).
The heterogeneity run (03) relaxes homogeneity across CITY CHARACTERISTICS
but not across TIME SINCE ADOPTION. An event-study parametrization
(leads and lags around the implementation year) would show the dynamic
path and provide a sharper pre-trend test than the single placebo dummy.

5. MEDIATORS ARE EXCLUDED -- TOTAL EFFECTS ONLY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The baseline deliberately excludes mediators (fleet composition, pm25), so
the estimates are TOTAL policy effects. This is the right choice for the
research question, but it means the analysis cannot decompose channels
(fleet renewal vs. reduced driving). The sensitivity row with mediators
included shows the expected attenuation and should be read as a
specification check, not as an alternative estimate.

6. STATISTICAL CAVEATS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- The synergy estimate rests on only 28 cities that ever run both policies
  (281 city-years). The CI is wide accordingly; small-sample cluster
  behavior in this cell deserves caution.
- CP alone is only borderline significant (p = 0.057); the data cannot
  distinguish a modest CP effect from zero once clustering is respected.
- Log outcome: effects are constant in PERCENT by construction. If policies
  cut absolute tonnage similarly across city sizes, the log specification
  would itself induce apparent heterogeneity by size.
- Multiple testing in the heterogeneity table (18 interactions): with ~18
  tests, one significant coefficient at the 5% level is expected by chance;
  the CP interaction pattern is credible mainly because it is jointly
  coherent (density, GDP, transit, fuel price all point the same way), not
  because any single p-value is small.

7. EXTERNAL VALIDITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The data are simulated European-style city panels. Magnitudes (a 54% joint
reduction) should not be transferred to real policy settings; the value of
the exercise is methodological -- in particular the demonstration that a
well-specified DML pipeline with strong estimator robustness can still
produce badly biased causal estimates when treatment assignment is
endogenous, and that a placebo/pre-trend design is what detects it.

8. WHAT WOULD STRENGTHEN THE DESIGN (next steps)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Add city fixed effects or a Mundlak device (city means of the controls)
  to absorb time-invariant selection, and/or condition on pre-treatment
  outcome levels.
- Event-study leads and lags around implementation: flat pre-coefficients
  would be direct evidence on pre-trends; the dynamic post path would test
  the constant-effect assumption.
- Exploit the announcement-to-implementation window explicitly (e.g.,
  estimate effects relative to the announcement period rather than to all
  untreated years), which nets out the level gap the placebo detects.
- An instrument for adoption timing (e.g., political shocks unrelated to
  emissions) would identify the effect without conditional ignorability;
  no such instrument is available in this dataset.
